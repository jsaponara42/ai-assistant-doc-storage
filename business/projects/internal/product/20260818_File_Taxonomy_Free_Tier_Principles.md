# File Taxonomy — Free-Tier Principles

The basics JC is comfortable giving away for free: how to *name and structure* files. Not migration execution, not ongoing maintenance/oversight, not the bespoke judgment work of mapping a specific company's actual chaos onto a schema — that stays the paid engagement (Layer 1 of the offering stack, see [[20260814_information-taxonomy-offering-stack]]).

**Origin:** First articulated for Ruthless For Good's SharePoint restructure (see [[../client-projects/26073101_ruthless-for-good/20260818_Discovery_Brief_Ruthless_For_Good]], P-005), generalized here since the underlying principles aren't RFG-specific. Flagged in the offering-stack doc as a candidate for the free taxonomy chart / DIY guide lead magnet — the reciprocation mechanic: give away the rules, sell the implementation and upkeep.

**The pitch this content sets up implicitly:** giving these away is safe because *knowing* the rules and *actually applying and holding the line on them* are different problems. The rules fit in a page. Applying them to years of accumulated organizational mess, and keeping a whole team from drifting back into chaos, is the hard part — and that's what stays paid.

## The core principles

### 1. Everything is a "Project"
Almost everything an organization stores belongs inside a Project folder — not just client engagements or initiatives with a clear end date. This includes:
- Client/portfolio/customer work (obviously a project)
- **Internal work** — same treatment, same rigor
- **Indefinitely ongoing things** — sales goals, an annual initiative, anything without a natural end date. These still become "Projects"; they just get **split into time-boxed increments** (quarterly or yearly) rather than living as one folder that never closes. Evergreen material that doesn't change increment-to-increment lives at the *base* of the folder, above the time-boxed subfolders, so it's written once and referenced by every increment rather than duplicated or left stale in last year's folder.

**Why:** a single consistent mental model ("if it's not evergreen infrastructure, it's a Project") removes the ambiguity that causes files to land in the wrong place in the first place. Ambiguity about where something belongs is where taxonomy drift starts.

### 2. One code or name per entity, used identically across every system
Each meaningfully distinct entity — a portfolio company, a client, a fund — gets **one referenceable code or name**, and that exact identifier is used the same way in every system that touches it: the shared drive, the CRM, the finance/accounting system, anywhere else. Not "Acme Corp" in one place and "Acme" or a deal ID in another.

**Why this matters more than it sounds like it should:** once every system uses the same identifier for the same entity, cross-system AI navigation stops being a hard problem. An AI assistant (or a person) can be pointed at "the Acme project" and reliably find the SharePoint folder, the CRM record, and the finance line without translation. Consistency here is what makes the eventual "AI connects everything" outcome cheap instead of requiring custom mapping work later.

### 3. Numerical codes for sorting and reference
Use a numerical code as part of the folder name — either at the **front** (for natural chronological/priority sorting) or at the **end**, separated by a dash or underscore (for readability when the name itself matters most at a glance). Pick one convention and hold it consistently across the whole system; which end the number goes on matters less than never mixing both.

**Why:** numerical prefixes/suffixes make folders sort predictably without anyone having to remember an arbitrary order, and they give a short, stable reference token that's faster to say, type, or search than a full name — especially useful once an AI assistant is doing the searching.

### 4. A real "Archive" culture
Stale information doesn't announce itself — it just quietly sits in an active folder, indistinguishable from current material, until someone (or something) trusts it by mistake. The fix isn't better search; it's a genuine habit of **moving things out**, into a clearly labeled Archive, the moment they stop being current.

**Why this is a culture problem, not a folder-structure problem:** the Archive folder itself is trivial to create. What's hard — and what actually prevents stale information — is the team consistently *using* it: noticing when something's gone stale and moving it, rather than leaving it in place because moving it takes ten extra seconds. This is a discipline, not a one-time setup task.

### 5. Enforcement is the whole game
None of the above survives contact with a real, busy team unless it's actually **upheld** — every time, not just when it's convenient. Left unenforced, any naming/structure convention degrades gradually: one exception becomes two, becomes "eh, close enough," becomes chaos again within a few months.

The honest framing: it's mildly annoying for the first couple of weeks, and then it becomes second nature — the same way any new habit does. The organizations that actually get the benefit are the ones that treat the convention as non-negotiable during that adjustment window, not the ones that relax it the first time it's inconvenient.

## What this section deliberately doesn't cover (stays paid)

- **Applying this to a specific company's actual mess.** These are the rules; mapping years of accumulated, inconsistent, multi-owner file history onto them is bespoke judgment work — every organization's chaos is a different chaos.
- **Migration execution.** Big-bang vs. duplicate-and-parallel-run vs. incremental — a real decision with real risk, not covered here (see the offering-stack doc's open question on this).
- **Ongoing oversight and drift prevention.** Point 5 above says enforcement is the whole game — but *how* that enforcement actually happens day to day (who checks, how often, what a drift-check even looks like) is the recurring, paid layer of this offering, not a free-tier deliverable.
- **Metadata schema and staff change-management** beyond the naming convention itself.
